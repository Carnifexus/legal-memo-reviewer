# Reviewing a Legal Memo as a Decision Document

## Abstract

A memo can sound persuasive yet leave its reader with the wrong decision. A consequential date may come from a spreadsheet instead of the underlying record. A case may be accurately quoted but address a different procedural question. A recommendation may depend on a fact nobody has checked. This workflow is designed to surface those defects before a lawyer relies on the memo. It works with paper, a spreadsheet, or an approved AI assistant; it does not require any particular vendor.

## Review the decision, not just the writing

Start by naming the decision, decision-maker, time horizon, and available alternatives. A client deciding whether to accept a commercial proposal needs a different review than counsel deciding whether to make a factual assertion in a filing. Record the draft version under review, the list of materials actually available, and any explicit scope limits. This lets a second reviewer tell the difference between a missing source and a source that was intentionally excluded.

Spend the first pass on assertions that could change the recommendation. A stylistic flaw, an unsupported dollar estimate, and a missing deadline are not equal findings. If everything is flagged as critical, the memo is no easier to use after review than before.

## Build an auditable chain

For each material assertion, record the precise document or authority and locator. Then ask what the source actually establishes, as opposed to what the author infers from it. A document may show that an entry was recorded, for example, without identifying who performed the act it describes. Apparent corroboration can also come from two records that repeat the same underlying entry.

Where law matters, reading an opinion or rule is distinct from verifying its present applicability. Check the official text where available, the operative version, the relevant passage, and the procedural context. Independently check whether later history, contrary authority, or controlling local rules change the analysis. Explain what remains unverified rather than treating access to a quotation as verification of the asserted proposition. The reviewer should not manufacture a citation, fill in a missing page, or claim that a search was run if it was not.

## Stress-test the recommendation

Write the best competing interpretation in terms a thoughtful opponent or skeptical decision-maker would recognize. Anchor it to the source, articulate what it implies, and name the fact or authority most likely to decide between the alternatives. A possible disadvantage is not necessarily a strong counterargument; the useful one explains why the current recommendation might fail on its own terms.

Weight findings by consequence: decision-changing, material, or cleanup. Report confidence separately. A low-confidence issue can justify immediate action if the cost of being wrong is substantial. Conversely, a certain typo rarely deserves top billing. Use numerical estimates only when there is an identified basis for them; the labels are not probabilities.

## Finish with a decision and a handoff

State one recommendation for the record as it stands. If the right decision is to defer a position, specify what to obtain, by when, and what evidence would change course. Assign each task a role and distinguish an internal target from any independently confirmed deadline. A report that only presents alternatives has not resolved the decision it set out to address.

The method is useful when another reviewer can reconstruct the outcome from the sources, repeat the checks, and see why one issue mattered more than another. It does not guarantee accuracy, preserve privilege by itself, or substitute for counsel’s judgment. Use only approved data handling, and keep access to confidential materials no broader than the matter requires.
